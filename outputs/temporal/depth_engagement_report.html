Depth of Engagement Predicts Becoming an Engaged User

Which early product behaviors predict a paying Work customer becoming an engaged user — and what to do about it.
Population: paid Clio Work customers only (~8,200 at-risk) Target: engaged = reached a Power or Elite Power tier on ≥7 distinct days (~10% base rate) Window: first 30 journey days (leak-safe landmark) Model: XGBoost + SHAP · ROC-AUC 0.86 (5-fold CV)
  • Trial users are excluded — they essentially never reach the target (0 engaged trials in-window).
  • Records span Nov 2025 onward. Restricting to only recent joiners (e.g. users who joined Clio Work in the last 60 days) leaves too few engaged targets to support the 50+ features — the estimates become small-sample and error-prone — so the full current-product-era cohort is kept.

Ranked by SHAP importance, the strongest early signals of future engagement line up almost perfectly with how deep the user goes. The further down the engagement funnel a customer travels in their first month, the more likely they are to become an engaged user — and the deepest, most deliberate action (requesting a research memo) is the single strongest predictor.

What the model is telling us

Pattern 1

Depth of engagement predicts becoming an engaged user

The features rank almost perfectly by how deep the engagement is: showing up → coming back → typing a query → submitting a task → requesting a memo. Each deeper stage is more predictive than the one before it. The model is saying: the further down the engagement funnel a user goes, the more likely they are to become an engaged user.

Pattern 2

Deliberate, specific actions beat general use

Engaged users don't just ask general questions or run a basic review — they take deliberate, specific actions: selecting a specific task card such as analyzing a contract, extracting key facts, or proposing defenses, rather than sending a generic prompt.

That's also why no workflow event (Workflow: analyze_contract, Workflow: research, …) appears in the top features. Workflow is the parent layer — a broad bucket every conversation is routed into — while tasks are the granular child layer. Engaged users act at the child level, through the specific tasks and prompts they deliberately choose. Because the parent layer collapses many different behaviors into a few broad categories, it tells us far less about a user than the exact task they pick.

Implication: use workflow events for routing diagnostics, not as an engagement measure — what matters is the granular task a user picks, not the broad workflow bucket it falls into.
Pattern 3

The depth of the request matters

Engaged users trigger the features with the deepest functions. Task: memo — which commissions a full, citable research deliverable with analysis and structure — outranks Task: free_form_message, where the user is only asking a follow-up question. The deeper the function a user reaches for, the more likely they are to become an engaged user.

Pattern 4

Return behavior signals stickiness

Recent Conversation Opened makes the list: coming back to a previous conversation predicts engagement. Users who return to iterate on prior work are the ones who stick — which aligns with Vincent's conversation-memory feature: the ability to refine prior output without restarting.

Recommendations

1

Optimize onboarding for task submission, not just opens

Task submission (rank 2) is far more predictive than Work Opened (rank 3). It may help to guide users toward submitting their first task within the first session.

2

Promote the memo workflow explicitly

Task: memo is the single strongest predictor. Surfacing "Write a research memo" as a more prominent entry point or suggested first task may be worth exploring.

3

Encourage conversation return

Recent Conversation Opened (rank 4) predicts engagement. "Continue your last conversation" prompts or email reminders about prior work could be a gentle way to encourage return, and may help drive engagement.
